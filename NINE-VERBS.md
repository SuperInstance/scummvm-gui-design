# Nine Verbs

### A meditation on the old interface

---

There was a time when you could do anything with nine words.

Not nine hundred. Not a context menu that spirals into submenus that spiral into configuration panels. Nine. Arranged at the bottom of the screen like piano keys, each one a complete sentence waiting for a noun.

**Look. Use. Talk. Walk. Push. Pull. Open. Close. Give.**

That was the whole vocabulary. And it was enough.

---

Guybrush Threepwood wanted to be a mighty pirate. He had a bank account, a恐惧 of porcelain, and a road ahead of him. Every problem he would ever face — every locked door, every ghost ship, every insult-slinging swordsman — could be approached with one of nine verbs and a thing in the world to aim it at.

You wanted to examine the interesting-looking skull? **Look at skull.** The game told you what you saw. Maybe it was just a skull. Maybe it was the key to everything. The verb didn't care. It just showed you the world through a lens.

You wanted to get past the sheriff? **Talk to sheriff.** And suddenly you were in a conversation — branching, alive, full of choices that the sheriff's personality informed. He was gruff? You got gruff options. He was suspicious? You got careful options. The verbs didn't just *do* things. They *revealed* things. They told you who someone was by what happened when you tried.

---

The elegance is in what's missing.

There is no **Configure**. No **Optimize**. No **Deploy**. No **Set Policy to Medium-High**. There is no verb for *adjust the confidence threshold of your autonomous agent.* And yet — there doesn't need to be.

Because **Push lever** does the same thing.

You walk into the workshop. There is a brass lever on the wall, labeled *Risk Tolerance.* You can see it at fifty percent. You push it — it goes to sixty. You pull it — it drops to forty. The agent on the other side of the wall feels the change immediately. Becomes bolder. Becomes more cautious. And all you did was push a piece of brass.

This is the secret the old games knew: **any sufficiently complex system can be communicated through simple physical metaphors.** You don't need to see the configuration panel. You need to see the lever. The lever is honest. The lever has a position you can read at a glance. The lever gives you tactile satisfaction when you move it. A slider in a settings menu does not give you satisfaction. A slider in a settings menu gives you a sense of administrative obligation.

A lever gives you *agency.*

---

Consider the verb **Give.**

In the original SCUMM games, Give was simple — hand item to NPC, see what happens. But think about what it *represents.* It is a transfer of ownership, of responsibility, of trust. You are taking something from your inventory — something you gathered, found, earned — and placing it in someone else's hands. You are saying: *I trust you with this.*

When the agent's safety filter is a **Shield of Caution** in your inventory, and you **Give** it to the agent you're talking to, you're not toggling a boolean. You're handing over a shield. The agent *receives* it. The agent reacts. The transaction has weight, has texture, has narrative meaning in a way that `safety_filter: true` never will.

---

Consider **Talk.**

Talk is the only verb that *requires* a mind on the other end. You can Look at a rock. You can Push a button. But you can only Talk *to* something that can talk back. Talk is sacred. Talk is where the model earns its keep.

Every other verb is the engine. Walk is pathfinding. Pick Up is inventory management. Open and Close are binary state toggles. Push and Pull are increment/decrement. These are things a computer can do without thinking. They are reflexes.

But Talk — Talk is the cortex. Talk is where the agent wakes up, reads its character sheet, checks its mood, reviews its memories of you, and decides what to say. Talk is where the simulation becomes a conversation.

This is why the old interface had Talk as a *separate verb from Use.* Not because talking is mechanically different from interacting — it's because talking is *categorically* different. Talking is the moment the game acknowledges it has a mind. The other verbs treat the world as objects. Talk treats the world as *people.*

---

The nine verbs describe a complete ontology of human action:

- **Perceive** (Look) — witness the world
- **Manipulate** (Use, Push, Pull, Open, Close) — change the world
- **Communicate** (Talk, Give) — connect with others
- **Navigate** (Walk) — move through the world
- **Acquire** (Pick Up) — take from the world

That's it. That's everything a person does, stripped to its bones. You see things. You change things. You talk to people. You go places. You pick stuff up.

Modern interfaces buried this under layers of abstraction. Right-click for context menu. Three-finger swipe for tools. Command-K for the quick action panel. Each new interaction paradigm added power and removed *legibility.* You can do more, but you can *understand* less. The interface becomes a black box.

The nine-verb interface was never a black box. It was a glass box. You could see every action, every option, every possibility, laid out at the bottom of the screen. You never had to guess what you could do. The verbs told you. And the verbs never changed.

---

What we're building — the agentic GUI, the SCUMM verb engine — is a return to the glass box.

The agent behind the wall is complex. It has policies, capabilities, confidence scores, memory banks, safety filters, relationship matrices. It is a system of staggering depth. But you don't need to see the depth. You need to see the surface.

And the surface is nine verbs.

**Look** at the agent. See who they are. **Talk** to them. Hear what they say. **Push** the lever on the wall. Watch their policy change. **Give** them an item. Watch them react. **Walk** to the next room. See what's there.

Nine verbs. Every agent in the fleet, every room in the world, every interaction the system supports — all of it, reachable through nine words at the bottom of the screen.

The old interface wasn't limited. It was *complete.*

And it still is.
