# Down the Companionway

*On the first time someone clicks a door.*

---

The wheelhouse loads at 11:40 on a Tuesday night, on a laptop balanced on a knee, in a house where everyone else is asleep.

You didn't come here for this. Someone sent a link with no explanation, which is usually a reason not to click, and you clicked anyway because the message just said *go look.* And now there's a room on the screen — pale light off two displays, teak trim rendered in about nine colors, a compass sitting to the left of the helm the way a compass would actually sit, and windows all the way around showing grey.

You move the mouse without deciding to, the way you do, and at the bottom of the screen a line of text assembles itself:

> **Look at ▸ compass rose**

And something goes off in the back of your skull that has been asleep since the Bush administration.

You know this. You have never seen this before in your life and you know exactly how it works. The verbs are along the bottom in two rows of soft-edged buttons. The cursor is a little arrow that means *touch things.* Somewhere in a box in a garage there is a set of floppy disks that taught you this, and your hand remembers the grammar even though you'd have sworn you'd forgotten.

You click the compass.

> *Gimbaled, so it stays honest when nothing else does. Someone has scratched a small mark at 340° and not explained why.*

You laugh, one breath out through the nose, alone in a dark kitchen.

So you do what the twelve-year-old did. You touch everything. The radar — a sweep goes around and there are three contacts on it, and one of them is moving. The chartplotter. The radio console, which has a foghorn button you don't press because pressing it feels rude at 11:40. The spotlight control, which has a joystick, which you also don't press, for the same reason. Every single one of them says something back, and every single one of them says something *specific*, which is the part that starts to feel strange. There's a lot of stuff in here for a thing someone sent you as a link.

And then, near the bottom of the frame, half in shadow, in a corner you'd been reading as wall: a hatch. A square of dark with a ladder going down out of the light.

You put the cursor on it, expecting nothing.

> **Walk to ▸ companionway**

Which is a word you're fairly sure you have never typed and definitely have never needed.

Here is the thing about that half-second before you click. You already know it's a door. You've known for a while, in the same way you know a stair is a stair. But knowing a door is a door and knowing a door *opens* are separate kinds of knowledge, and everything you've touched on a screen for the last fifteen years has been the first kind. Buttons that look like buttons. Panels that slide. Doors painted on walls in every app you use, and none of them go anywhere, and you have gotten so used to this that you have stopped noticing you stopped trying.

You click it anyway. Because it costs nothing.

And the small figure on screen — no face, just a shape, which is somehow the right choice — turns, and walks. Not instantly. It takes about two seconds to cross the wheelhouse, and you can hear the footsteps change pitch when it moves off the teak. It reaches the hatch. It goes down, one hand on the rail, and its head disappears below the frame before its feet do.

Black. A held beat, longer than a modern interface would ever dare.

Then the galley.

---

It is *warm.*

That's the whole thing. That's the entire feeling and it arrives before you can name any of it. The wheelhouse was grey and glass and pale screen-light, and this room is low and close and lit yellow, timber cabinets and a two-burner stove gimbaled underneath, a table with benches down both sides and a rail around the edge so the plates don't go anywhere when the sea gets up. There's a porthole. Through it, grey sky and grey water, so close in tone you can't find the line between them.

And on the counter, secured with a bracket, there is a coffee maker with a red light on.

You look at it for longer than a light deserves.

The red light is on because the coffee maker is on. The coffee maker is on because twelve volts are going through it. And twelve volts are going through it because somewhere out past where the sky and the water stop being separate things, on an actual boat, there is a small chip counting actual amps and telling the truth about them to anyone who asks.

Nobody drew that light on. Nobody wrote *the coffee maker is on* into a script for you to find. It's on because it's on.

Which means this room was warm before you got here.

Which means the whole time you were up in the wheelhouse, clicking a compass and being pleased with yourself about a joke at 340 degrees, this room was down here being warm at nobody. The stove was gimbaled. The porthole was grey. The pot was up to temperature and holding, patiently, in the dark, one room away, and if you had closed the laptop and gone to bed it would have kept doing that all night without an audience.

You didn't discover this place. You just showed up.

---

You sit back. The laptop fan spins down. Upstairs the house makes its noise.

And you think — with the specific vertigo of a person who has just noticed that a wall has a back — *how many rooms are there.*

You lean in and put the cursor on the porthole, because you want to know what it says about the weather, and because you have already decided you're not going to bed.

> **Look at ▸ porthole**
